What you can measure once you have ground truth

The soccer pipeline currently makes three claims it cannot support, all for the same reason: there is nothing to check against. This is what SoccerNet and a labelled detection set actually buy — and why each fix is structural rather than a better guess.

§01

Three claims with nothing behind them

Not hypothetical problems. Each of these is a line already written into the projects, and each is honest about being a proxy — but a documented proxy is still a proxy.

The claimWhat's actually behind itWhy it doesn't hold
"Tracking is working — 12 IDs, mean track length 81.3 frames" Counting IDs and averaging track lengths Track length rises when tracking improves and when the detector simply finds fewer marginal objects. Two causes, one number.
"The minimap looks right" Looking at the minimap A homography fitted to badly placed corners produces a plausible-looking map that is wrong by metres. Plausibility is not accuracy.
"Teams are assigned correctly" k-means with k=2 on crop embeddings Goalkeepers and referees wear a third and fourth kit. With two clusters they must be misassigned — the error is structural, not tuning.

The through-line is the same failure that has now appeared three times in this portfolio: a single number that two competing explanations both predict. It showed up in the ByteTrack sweep, where unique-ID count couldn't separate fragmentation from genuine extra detections. It showed up in the Kalman figure, where mean error was identical at both extremes of the gain. It is here again.

The fix is never a cleverer proxy. It is data that already knows the answer.

§02

Why one tracking number was never going to work

Once you have ground-truth tracks, the obvious move is to compute the standard metrics. There are two classical ones, and the interesting thing is that they disagree with each other by construction.

MOTA counts mistakes per frame

MOTA = 1 − (FN + FP + IDSW) / GT

Missed detections, false positives and identity switches, over the total number of ground-truth detections. Note the shape: identity switches are one term of three, and in practice the smallest by an order of magnitude. Ten players tracked across a hundred frames give a thousand ground-truth detections; even swapping every player's identity once contributes just ten to that numerator.

MOTA is therefore a detection metric wearing a tracking metric's name.

IDF1 asks who kept their name

IDF1 = 2·IDTP / (2·IDTP + IDFP + IDFN)

Computed after a global one-to-one matching between ground-truth trajectories and predicted trajectories: each real player is assigned the single predicted track that best covers them, and everything else counts against you. Split one player across two IDs and only the longer half is credited — regardless of how good the boxes were.

The consequence for benchmarking

These two metrics can rank the same pair of trackers in opposite orders. A tracker that detects conservatively but never confuses identities loses on MOTA and wins on IDF1. Quoting whichever one flatters your result is the single easiest way to publish a meaningless comparison — and because both are real, standard metrics, nothing about it looks wrong.

§03

HOTA, and the number that should change how you think about this

The modern answer, and what SoccerNet's evaluation server actually reports, is HOTA — Higher Order Tracking Accuracy. It refuses the trade-off by measuring detection and association separately and then combining them:

HOTA = √(DetA × AssA) DetA — detection accuracy: did you find the objects? AssA — association accuracy: did you keep their identities?

The geometric mean is the point. An arithmetic mean lets a great detector paper over hopeless association; a geometric mean does not, because either factor going to zero takes the whole score with it. You cannot buy HOTA with detection alone.

Worth checking yourself

Published state of the art on SoccerNet-Tracking: HOTA 85.443, DetA 99.236, AssA 73.567. And √(0.99236 × 0.73567) = 0.85443 — exactly the reported figure. The relationship isn't an approximation being offered to you here; it reproduces the benchmark number to five decimal places.

Now read those three numbers again, because they carry the real lesson. Those methods were given oracle detections by the dataset — perfect boxes, handed over for free. DetA is 99.2, as you'd expect when detection is solved by fiat.

AssA is still only 73.6.

Hand a state-of-the-art soccer tracker flawless boxes and better than a quarter of the association work is still wrong. Players on a team look alike, the camera pans constantly, and bodies occlude each other every few seconds. Identity — not detection — is the hard problem in this domain, and no amount of detector fine-tuning touches it.

For the soccer project this reframes the roadmap. The open items were "fine-tune a detector" and "add ball tracking" — both detection work. The benchmark says the ceiling is on the association side, which means re-identification features, better motion models through occlusion, and the second association pass ByteTrack already does.

§04

Calibration you can measure instead of assert

SoccerNet's calibration set annotates the pitch itself: every visible field segment — lines, circle arcs, goalposts — marked as polylines across 22,816 images, and processed geometrically into 57 keypoints including the tangent points of the centre-circle ellipse.

What that changes is not the homography. It's that you can finally put a number on it.

TodayWith field annotations
CorrespondencesFour corners placed by eyeUp to 57 annotated keypoints
Fit qualityExactly determined — no residual existsOver-determined — least squares leaves a residual you can read
Error checkLook at the minimapReprojection error, in metres
Bad-frame handlingUndetectedRANSAC rejects outlier correspondences automatically

The over-determination is the part worth dwelling on. Four points give you exactly eight equations for eight unknowns, so the fit is perfect by construction — the residual is zero no matter how badly you placed them. There is no signal in it. Add a fifth point and the system can disagree with itself, and that disagreement is the error estimate you never had.

Same bug, one project earlier

Traffic Lens under-reported every speed by ~8× because a road quad was assumed to be 7 m × 30 m when it was 25 m × 250 m. Nothing crashed, and the annotated video looked entirely normal. It was caught by a human asking whether 55 km/h was plausible on an empty motorway. A pitch with annotated keypoints turns that judgement call into a residual — the same class of error, prevented rather than noticed.

§05

Explicit classes dissolve a problem clustering can't solve

Roboflow's football-players-detection set labels four classes — ball, player, goalkeeper, referee — with pretrained YOLOv8/v9/v11 and RF-DETR checkpoints available.

The interesting thing is which kind of fix that is. The goalkeeper problem is not a bad hyperparameter. Two clusters cannot represent four kits; no value of any tuning knob makes k=2 correct. Detecting goalkeepers and referees as their own classes doesn't tune the clustering — it removes them from the clustering's input entirely, leaving it with the one job it can actually do.

Worth naming the distinction, because it recurs: a parameter fix makes a wrong model less wrong; a structural fix makes the model right. Raising k is the former. Changing what enters the clustering is the latter.

The same dataset also gives ball as a first-class label. A COCO model's sports ball class was trained mostly on large, centred, unblurred balls — nothing like a 10-pixel motion-blurred football, which is the hardest object in the pipeline.

The honest costs, since the point of this series is not to skip them:

  • Access takes a step. SoccerNet is distributed through its own toolkit and requires agreeing to terms before download — check the current conditions yourself rather than trusting a summary, including whether your intended use is covered.
  • It is large. 200 sequences of 30 s plus a 45-minute halftime video for long-term tracking — 225,375 annotated frames. Plan storage and start with a subset.
  • Formats need conversion. MOT-style annotations into whatever your evaluation harness expects. TrackEval is the standard implementation and is worth using rather than reimplementing — these metrics have edge cases you don't want to rediscover.
  • A benchmark number is a commitment. Once you can be compared, you will be. That's the entire value.
The order to do this in

Fine-tune on the Roboflow set first — it's small, immediate, and fixes the goalkeeper and ball problems structurally. Then bring in SoccerNet for evaluation, because that's the step that turns "the tracking looks good" into a number a stranger can check. Detection first because it's cheap; measurement second because it's what makes the rest defensible.
